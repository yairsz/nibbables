Bully stick vs rawhide: what veterinary sources and 14 brands say

Bully sticks and rawhide are made from different parts of the animal and go through different processing, according to the veterinary sources quoted below. This page puts those definitions next to what 14 bully stick brands' own product pages say about rawhide, word for word. Five pages say their stick is rawhide-free or has no rawhide, five more describe it as an alternative to rawhide or compare it with rawhide, and four don't mention rawhide at all. Nibbables doesn't rate chews; ask your vet what suits your dog.

Last checked 2026-10-30

Before you give any chew: supervise your dog while it chews, choose a size and hardness that suits your dog (follow the brand's size guidance), take away small or broken pieces, keep fresh water available, and ask your vet if a chew is right for your dog, especially for puppies, seniors, dogs with dental work or dogs on special diets. Bully stick vs rawhide for dogs: what's the difference?

The sources describe two different products. Rawhide is hide: Today's Veterinary Practice says “Rawhide chews are made from the chemically processed inner hide layer, most commonly from cows but also other animal species (e.g., pigs, sheep, even water buffalo).” A bully stick is a pizzle: Tufts' Petfoodology blog says “Bully sticks are, in fact, bull or steer penises.” Today's Veterinary Practice also files them differently, listing bully sticks under “Animal-derived products that have undergone minimal processing” and rawhides among “extensively processed animal-derived chews”. The AKC adds that “Unlike rawhide,” “bully sticks, sometimes known as pizzle sticks, are easily digestible.”

Are bully sticks rawhide?

No. None of the sources we checked calls a bully stick rawhide. The ASPCA says “Rawhide chews are manufactured from animal hides.” The 14 bully stick pages in the table list beef pizzle (or, on Mighty Paw's page, “Ingredients: Beef”) as the ingredient, and Jack's Premium spells it out: “They are made from 100% steer or bull penis, also commonly referred to as pizzle.” A 2013 study in the Canadian Veterinary Journal lists rawhide and bully sticks as separate treats: “cattle hooves or skin (i.e., rawhide chews)” and “the bull or steer penis, commonly known as “bully” or “pizzle” sticks.”

Are all bully sticks rawhide free?

Of the 14 bully stick pages we checked, 5 say so in plain words: GREAT DOG CO. (“rawhide-free”), Nature Gnaws (“rawhide-free chew”), Redbarn (“No Rawhide”), Sancho & Lola's Closet (“They will never contain any rawhide.”) and Jack's Premium (“They are bone and rawhide free.”). Five more (Best Bully Sticks, Jack&Pup, Bow Wow Labs, Pawstruck and Bully Bunches) describe their stick as an alternative to rawhide or compare it with rawhide without saying “rawhide-free”. Four pages (Campfire Treats, Mighty Paw, Pupford and Raw Paws Pet Food) don't mention rawhide in their own text. All 14 list pizzle or beef as the ingredient. If the wording matters to you, ask the brand.

Are bully sticks or rawhide better for dogs?

Nibbables doesn't rank chews, so here is what the sources say. On digestion, the AKC says “Rawhide is also difficult to digest. Pieces will pass through the digestive system whole, which is why they pose an obstruction risk.” and that bully sticks are “easily digestible”. Today's Veterinary Practice says “The digestibility of rawhide chews is often unknown or suspected to be poor”. Both have cautions: for rawhide the AKC says “Power chewers can break off large pieces.”, and for bully sticks “power chewers might bite straight through, producing large pieces that pose a choking risk, or swallow the entire stick whole.” Redbarn puts the brand view this way: “Many pet parents choose bully sticks as an alternative to rawhide because they are single-ingredient chews and digestible when fed appropriately.” Your vet knows your dog and can tell you what suits it.

How rawhide is made, as Today's Veterinary Practice describes it

The article, by two board-certified veterinary nutritionists, says: “To produce a rawhide chew, the hide may be processed in brine (salt), degreasers, detergents, sodium bicarbonate, hydrogen peroxide, lime, or other chemicals needed to remove hair or fat, kill pathogens, and improve the appearance.” It adds: “Artificial colors and flavors are often added to make the final product more palatable, and additives (e.g., glues to hold shape) may be used.”

None of the 14 bully stick pages in the table lists anything besides pizzle or beef as an ingredient. Sancho & Lola's Closet writes: “All of our chews are 100% natural and digestible. They will never contain any rawhide.” Redbarn's FAQ says: “A bully stick is a single-ingredient dog chew made from 100% beef pizzle.”

What studies say about bully sticks themselves

Rawhide isn't the only chew with open questions. The 2013 Canadian Veterinary Journal study measured bully sticks: “Mean caloric density was 15 kcal/inch (38 kcal/cm) [range: 9 to 22 kcal/inch (23 to 56 kcal/cm), 2.96 to 3.07 kcal/g].” It also reported: “Of 26 bully sticks that were tested for bacterial contamination 1 (4%) was contaminated with Clostridium difficile, 1 was contaminated with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), and 1 with a tetracycline resistant Escherichia coli.” The study didn't test rawhide. Our bully stick calories page lists what each brand states.

Labels and regulation

Today's Veterinary Practice notes that “Minimally processed bones, other body parts, extensively processed rawhides, and compressed rawhides are not considered “food” by the FDA and are therefore poorly regulated.” Writing about plant-based chews, it adds: “These may be marketed as “rawhide free,” but selecting a quality product is difficult because these chews are poorly regulated.” In the study it cites, “Of the 10 products tested, 2 contained skin layers (consistent with rawhides) and were therefore deemed inconsistent with their labeling.” The article doesn't name those products, and they weren't described as bully sticks.

Supervision, as the sources put it

For rawhide, the ASPCA says: “Pet parents should limit the amount of rawhide consumed at any given time and discourage consumption of significant pieces that could cause choking or gastrointestinal obstruction.” For bully sticks, the AKC says: “When your dog has chewed the stick down to a nub, take it away before they can swallow what’s left.” Best Bully Sticks: “Always supervise your dog when they're enjoying a bully stick, and remove small or broken pieces to prevent your pup from choking.” Redbarn: “Always choose a size suitable for your dog's chewing style and remove small end pieces that could become a choking hazard.” The ASPCA also “encourages guardians to actively observe their dog while chewing and to immediately consult a veterinarian if their pet consumes a chunk of chew material.”
